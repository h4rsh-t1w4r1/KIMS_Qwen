Read this alongside SKILL.md steps 1 to 3 when the subject is a clinic, hospital, dental practice, physio center, diagnostic lab, or any medical practitioner. It does not replace those phases. It changes three decisions inside them: what the hero motion is FOR, how long the journey should be, and what the copy is allowed to claim.

## Why this vertical is not a travel or lifestyle brand

The twelve laws in `prompt-laws.md` still apply exactly as written. What changes is the emotional job the hero is doing. A travel or perfume or coffee hero sells a fantasy: the visitor wants to feel transported. A patient looking at a clinic site is not shopping for a feeling, they are reducing a risk. They are anxious, they want competence signals, and they will leave the instant the site feels like spectacle for its own sake instead of evidence of a well-run practice. The halo effect (a visitor's first 50 milliseconds coloring everything after) still applies, but the thing it needs to signal is different: not "this is a beautiful escape" but "this is a clean, modern, competent, established place, run by real qualified people."

So: never propose a fantasy-journey concept (mist through a doorway into an abstract world, a product floating in space) as the hero for a clinic unless the practice's own world genuinely gives you that footage. Propose concepts sampled from the CLINIC'S actual world, per the existing Phase 3 rule, just interpreted through a competence-and-calm lens instead of a wonder lens.

## Translating the hero motion by sub-vertical

Law 1 still governs (the motion must agree with the scroll: down, opening, arriving). Choose the subject from this list, or something equally concrete to the specific practice, never a generic stock-medical look:

| Sub-vertical | What the motion should evoke | A concrete hero idea | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dental clinic | Precision, cleanliness, calm (not the drill, not the chair) | Soft natural light moving across a spotless, modern treatment room, arriving on a calm, uncluttered reception or a genuine smile (only with consent, see below) | Close-up of dental instruments, an open mouth, anything clinical-looking up close (anatomy law 5: everyone knows what a smile looks like wrong) |
| Multi-specialty hospital | Scale, being real and findable, established | A slow descent or approach from a wide establishing view down to the actual building's entrance | A generic glossy "hospital corridor" stock look with no connection to the real building |
| Ortho / physio / rehab | Restored movement, ease after difficulty | Motion built around fluid, unforced movement: light through a window onto a clean therapy space, or an object in smooth uninterrupted motion (a pendulum, water, fabric) standing in for "movement returning" | Literal before/after body shots, anything implying a medical outcome (see claims section below) |
| Skin / derma / aesthetic | Clarity, light, calm confidence | Soft, even light arriving on a clean surface or a calm interior; light itself is a forgiving subject (law 5) | Literal skin close-ups, any implied before/after |
| Diagnostic / pathology lab | Precision, speed, trust in a process | Abstract precision motion: light through glass, a clean instrument surface, or a straightforward descent into the actual lab space | Overused "DNA helix" and "data stream" clichés; they read as generic stock exactly like the AI-cliché palettes `scrub-pipeline.md` already warns against |

If the practice has real interior photos, prefer them as start frames over anything generated. A real clinic photographed well beats a beautiful generated fantasy every time in this vertical, because the whole point is proving the place is real.

## Depth tier: default to Tier 1, and mean it

Tier 1 (one 6-second shot) is the right default for almost every clinic, not just the cheap one. A patient is not browsing for inspiration, they came to find a doctor's name, a service, and a way to book. A long chained journey (Tier 2/3) makes them wait through spectacle before they get what they came for, and that works against you in this vertical specifically. Reserve Tier 2 for the rare case where the concept genuinely serves the patient: a large hospital walking the visitor from the building's exterior into the actual facility, chapter by chapter, IS useful information (it previews the place), not just visual flourish. Everything smaller than that stays Tier 1.

## What has to be true below the fold, and fast

Apply the "beautiful is the entry fee, converting is the $10,000" rule from Phase 3 with this vertical's specific trust furniture, in roughly this priority order, higher on the page than you would place it for a lifestyle brand:

1. Services offered, in plain patient language, not internal medical jargon.
2. The doctor(s): real name, real qualification, years in practice. This is the single highest-trust element on a clinic site. If the clinic will not give you this, ask again before building around a placeholder.
3. A direct way to act: call button, WhatsApp button, or booking link, repeated more than once on the page (a patient does not want to hunt for it).
4. Location and hours, ideally with a map. Do not make this feel like an afterthought footer.
5. Real testimonials only, exactly as the general skill already requires. This vertical raises the stakes on this rule: a fabricated review or a borrowed one (see the brand-coherence check in `scrub-pipeline.md`) on a medical practice is a much worse failure than on a coffee shop.

## What the copy is not allowed to claim

This is the one place this vertical differs from every other build in a way that matters beyond craft. In India, registered medical practitioners are bound by professional-conduct rules around self-promotion (historically under Indian Medical Council / National Medical Commission regulations) that restrict things like soliciting patients through claims of superiority, publishing patient testimonials framed as outcome guarantees, or advertising specific success rates. This skill is not legal advice and you are not a compliance officer, but hold the copy to a simple, safe standard regardless:

- State facts the clinic gives you: services offered, qualifications, timings, years established. Never invent a number (a patient count, a success rate, a review count) that was not given to you.
- Never write or imply a guaranteed medical outcome ("pain-free," "permanent results," "100% success").
- Never use a before/after claim, image, or implied comparison without the clinic's explicit sign-off, and prefer not to use one at all.
- If the clinic wants to display registration numbers, awards, or specific claims, ask them to confirm the exact wording and source rather than paraphrasing medical claims yourself.
- When in doubt, describe the practice and its people, not outcomes. "Dr. X has practiced dentistry in Nalasopara for twelve years" is safe and still lands the credibility point; "Dr. X will fix your smile permanently" is a promise you have no standing to make on their behalf.

Say this plainly to the client during Phase 2 if they ask for stronger claims than this: it protects them, not just the build.

## The pitch to the clinic itself (for Verro' own outreach, not the site copy)

The value case for an animated hero in this vertical is not "it looks cool," it is the halo effect stated honestly: patients decide competence in seconds, and every competing clinic without a modern site is losing that first impression by default. Lead the pitch with that, backed by the real, measured deploy numbers from `deploy.md` (the actual load-speed receipts), not with the word "animated" as if motion itself were the selling point. A clinic owner does not care that the hero scrubs with scroll; they care that it makes them look like the most credible option in a two-second glance, loads fast on a patient's phone, and gets a booking. Sell that, and let the craft be the reason it is true.
